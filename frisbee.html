Why won’t my disc fly straight?

A round at Valbyparken, some very questionable throws, and a reason to open STAR-CCM+.

STAR-CCM+Free flightRotating disc

How it started

Seven throws. Then two.

I was at Valbyparken in Copenhagen with colleagues, playing disc golf. The idea is simple: get a disc into a metal basket with hanging chains in as few throws as possible.

To be honest, we were particularly bad at it. Seven throws? Usually more. Our discs veered sideways, tilted, fell short and occasionally seemed determined to come back like boomerangs.

Then three players arrived with bags that looked as though they contained fifty discs. Two throws, and they were done. Their discs seemed to travel fifty metres in a straight line, with none of the wobbling and sideways detours we were producing.

How could I throw like that?

I had two options: spend twenty hours practising at Valbyparken, or ask my old friend STAR-CCM+ to help me understand what was happening. I chose the simulation first. Practice is still on the list.

This project explores how rotation, pressure and aerodynamic forces shape a disc’s flight. The first case is deliberately simple: a horizontal driver launched straight ahead.

The equipment

Why a bag full of discs?

Putters, mid-range discs and drivers serve different shots. Their rim shapes and flight characteristics help explain why experienced players carry more than one. This study uses a driver geometry.

A moving overset mesh follows the disc within the background domain. The red region below is the overset mesh. The base size is 10 mm, with local refinement down to 2 mm.

02 — Flow around the disc

Rotation, velocity and pressure

Rotation adds to the forward surface velocity on one side and subtracts from it on the other. These surface snapshots show the resulting asymmetry in X velocity and total pressure.

Total pressure includes a kinetic contribution, so this contrast is not by itself a lateral suction force. The force on the disc comes from the complete static-pressure and wall-shear distribution.

The upper surface shows extensive suction; the underside contains both positive and negative pressure regions. Together, these loads contribute to lift, which helps the disc stay airborne while drag slows it down.

The disc rises slightly, then descends as it travels forward. Starting from an assumed height of 1 m, it reaches 1.047 m and finishes the available record about 5 cm above ground. It has travelled 52.42 m, but the record does not yet include ground contact: this is recorded distance, not a measured landing range.

The flight stays close to its initial direction, with only about 6 cm of lateral displacement. Meanwhile, the forward speed decreases from 25 to 15.21 m/s — roughly a 39% reduction. This idealized release is much more controlled than my throws in the park.

Lift and drag during flight

The reported drag coefficient decreases as the disc slows. The lift coefficient oscillates around a more stable level. Over the final 0.20 s, their time-averaged values are Cd ≈ 0.0197 and Cl ≈ 0.0826.

Small periodic variations remain in the signals. Rotation-related loading and moving-mesh interpolation are possible contributors; these curves alone do not establish gyroscopic precession.

Force histories and calculation details

C = F / (½ρUref²Aref), with ρ = 1.18415 kg/m³, Uref = 25 m/s and Aref = 0.0314 m². The normalization is 11.6195 N; reference pressure is 0 Pa gauge. The final-window mean reported X and Z forces are 0.229 N and 0.959 N, respectively. The weight is 0.981 N.

The report axes and signs are retained as exported. Fixed-reference coefficients are not instantaneous-speed aerodynamic polars. For display only, values above three times the post-0.05 s 95th percentile of absolute magnitude are masked: 9 samples for drag/X force and 5 for lift/Z force. Raw data are unchanged.

Back to the park

Understanding the flight is a start.

This first simulation follows a clean horizontal release. It shows how a spinning driver can travel more than fifty metres while losing speed and height. Understanding less controlled throws will require looking at release orientation and aerodynamic moments. Turning that understanding into a good throw still takes practice.
